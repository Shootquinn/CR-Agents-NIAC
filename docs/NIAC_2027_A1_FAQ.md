1. In the Quad Chart, if we follow the suggested format in general, is that sufficient or is 
there more precise requirement (like the shading and size of each box)? 
Proposers should not provide the Proposal Quad Chart/Summary Chart described in the 
REDDI NRA, but must provide the Overview Chart, as described in the NIAC Appendix, 
which serves an equivalent purpose. 
 
2. Step A submission requires 3 pages of technical and management; what other information 
is desired? If we add a list of references to a Step A or Step B proposal, will these count 
against proposal length maximums? [updated 10/15/2026] 
Step A submissions include NSPIRES cover page information, an overview chart, the 3-
page technical and management section, and references (optional). For Step A and B, 
references are optional and may include as many pages as needed. They will not count 
against the page limits. Step A submission requirements are detailed in Section D.2 of 
the NIAC appendix. 
 
3. Past NIAC grants seem to have gone primarily to individual researchers to allow time to 
work an important question. You call them "Fellows" and seem to want one- person 
efforts. But with NASA money for early-stage work so exceedingly scarce, would you 
consider joint proposals where a group forms with diverse expertise to tackle a large 
subject? 
Proposals should comprise the necessary expertise and credentials to perform the work 
outlined in the work plan. Team members need to be identified only in the Step B 
submission. Multiple proposals addressing separate aspects of a single problem will be 
considered separately and must individually meet the terms of the solicitation. Joint 
(coordinated) proposals cannot be a mechanism for exceeding the $225K per study limit. 
 
4. How many concept papers can be submitted as a Step A? 
There is no limit. If this privilege is abused, a limit may be established in the future.

5. NIAC appears interested in TRL 1-2 ideas but will manufacturing readiness level be also 
considered? 
Any novel approach may be considered if put in the context of a reference mission and if 
the change results in a substantial improvement in mission capability. 
 
6. If a concept provides no new capability but dramatically reduces cost or dramatically 
increases lifetime will that be of interest? [updated 10/15/2026] 
It is possible that a much-improved approach to NASA mission implementation can score 
well in both innovation and impact, one or the other, or neither. It depends on what the 
innovation is and how it is incorporated into the mission. Reviewers will assess the 
proposal for its use of innovation and the resulting impact that innovation has on the 
mission being described. It is possible to do well in both categories. Cost reduction and 
technology lifetime extension are examples of potential impact. However, as stated in 
Sections C.2. and C.3 of the NIAC Appendix, “Innovations that simply exploit unrelated 
but anticipated future capability, without introducing a novel or unanticipated aspect may 
be considered incremental” and thus potentially out-of-scope for NIAC. 
 
7. I am an unaffiliated individual and therefore do not have any organizational data. Is there 
a work-around to this requirement or is this an absolute? 
Individuals are welcome to submit concepts, but they must be registered in NSPIRES. 
This means they must establish at least a “sole proprietorship” and then meet the 
NSPIRES requirements for an “authorizing organization.” Registration information is 
available at: 
https://nspires.nasaprs.com/external/externalhelp/public/index.htm#t=Registration_Infor
mation.htm

NIAC point to representative mission case studies to use as references for describing the 
technology's implementation? [updated 10/15/2026] 
Yes, please refer to previously Funded Studies, with particular focus on more recent 
awards at: https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/spacetech/niac/NIAC_funded_studies.html 
 
9. I’m a “garage inventor.” How can I use the funds if I’m awarded? 
Proposers may structure the $225,000 limit as needed to perform the proposed work plan. 
It may be used for study-related expenses such as labor, materials, and travel. A detailed 
budget is only required in the Step B submission.

11. For the Step A submission, do I just put myself down as the Principal Investigator (PI) or 
should I put my affiliation down as well? 
You must list both the PI and the PI’s affiliation in the NPIRES Cover Page information. 
 
12. Can I take a pre-existing NASA Mission and submit a way to enhance it? 
Yes, if submitting such an enhancement provides a substantial improvement to a pre- 
existing NASA mission. 
 
13. Previously, the original NASA Institute for Advanced Concepts included research 
without immediately apparent applications, and it was difficult to be picked as a Fellow. 
How has the selection process changed now that NIAC has been absorbed within NASA? 
NIAC now permits funding for NASA civil servants (the original NIAC did not). There is 
a formal review process consistent with NASA Legal and Procurement regulations. Also 
with the Step B Invitation criteria listed in the solicitation, reviewers are much stricter on 
enforcing them, and concepts MUST have a representative mission. 
 
14. Can we take cover page info and expand on it in the Technical Section? 
The technical section, and for Step B proposals the management section, must be 
complete and standalone without reference back to the cover page information. So, yes, 
you should repeat and expand on the cover page information to convey what is needed to 
meet the NIAC appendix requirements and to make a compelling argument for the 
proposed concept. 
 
15. I work at a NASA Center and I’ve already developed a concept and I want to expand on 
it. Can I do this with a NIAC proposal? [updated 10/15/2026] 
If the concept is being applied to a mission in a way that has not previously been 
explored and the proposer can convince the review panel that it's something that should 
be further explored in a NIAC effort, then that is acceptable. If the concept has been 
developed to the degree expected of a NIAC Phase I study, it would not be eligible for 
NIAC. For the proposal to be deemed eligible, there must be a lot to be gained from the 
proposed concept study, it must be substantially innovative, and it must also be placed in 
a representative mission. 
 
16. What’s the difference between a representative mission and a technology concept? 
A representative mission involves the architecture, science objectives, operations, 
logistics, system-level design, etc. that frames the innovation being proposed. Innovative 
technologies, such as an instrument or detector, should not be submitted as a standalone 
development in NIAC. Rather, they should be incorporated within a representative 
mission. To summarize, NIAC studies provide the opportunity to convince NASA that an

## Page 4

underlying technology concept would be worth further development by showing that it 
enables something new, substantially better, or more cost effective. 
 
17. If you have a Decadal Survey Mission and you want to build a new instrument with a 
new technology for it, is this within scope? 
Yes, if the proposed instrument is framed around the decadal survey mission and it 
provides significant gain in capabilities or substantially reduced total mission cost. If it is 
just an alternative way to accomplish the mission objective, then it may be out-of-scope. 
 
18. What are the rules for the NIAC program in relation to retaining companies’ intellectual 
property? If we win a Phase I award and perform work, can we protect our IP somehow? 
For a Step A proposal involving pre-existing, unpatented background intellectual 
property, what portions of the submission should a proposer assume may become public, 
and what mechanism, if any, should be used to identify proprietary background technical 
information while still providing reviewers sufficient detail to evaluate innovation and 
feasibility? [updated 10/15/2026] 
Proposals are considered to be protected information during the review. If selected for 
award, the program will likely publicly release the overview chart and a summary of the 
proposal. Throughout this process, Fellows retain IP rights. However, with rare 
exceptions, at the end of a funded study, Fellows should be prepared to publish all data in 
a detailed final report for public release. Also, please note that a brief Data Management 
Plan is required for proposal submission, through the Program Specific Data questions in 
the NSPIRES Cover Page information. Any protection (e.g., patents) should be sought 
before the final report is due. If there is very sensitive material that cannot be released, it 
may be included as a “not-for- release” appendix to the final report. 
 
19. One of the team members, is a Foreign National; is there a specific statement that must be 
added to the proposal to indicate their involvement? Are there any other formalities to be 
fulfilled? 
Involvement from foreign nationals is permitted and can be indicated with a letter of 
support from the individual in the Step B proposal. The Step B proposal must contain a 
certification that a sponsoring foreign government agency or foreign institution commits 
to bear the cost of the research proposed to be performed by the non-U.S. organization 
should the proposal be selected by NASA. Be aware that this is a U.S. Government 
procurement, and participation with foreign entities must be on a no-exchange-of-funds 
basis. If your foreign national team member is employed by a U.S. entity, the no-
exchange-of-funds basis would not apply. See Section B.3 of the SpaceTech-REDDI-
2025 NRA for further information.

32. Can we switch PI’s between Step A and Step B? 
You may change PI between Step A and B. Please note that approval and implementation 
of such a request will require additional process steps, and we recommend initiating the 
request by contacting hq-niac@mail.nasa.gov as soon as possible to help ensure

33. Can we switch institutions between Step A and Step B? 
Proposals to NIAC solicitations are submitted by organizations. If a Step A proposal is 
invited for Step B submission and the proposing team then wishes to change the 
submitting organization, NIAC will require confirmation prior to approving any changes. 
This at a minimum includes confirmation from the Step A Principal Investigator (PI) and 
the Step A Authorized Organizational Representative (AOR). 
While subsequent NSPIRES submission by the new organization can be facilitated by the 
NSPIRES Help Desk, it is a non-trivial process that can require considerable time and 
effort. This is especially true if the submitting organization is a new entity, requiring a 
new Unique Entity Identifier (UEI) from Sam.gov. We recommend initiating such a 
request by contacting hq-niac@mail.nasa.gov as soon as possible to help ensure 
submission of the Step B proposal by the deadline. 
 
34. If you are not associated with NASA, a university or a business can you still apply? 
Yes, you may still propose. You would need to have an affiliation with an organization 
with a unique Entity Identifier (UEI) Number registered in SAM.gov. You can also 
create your own organization and get a UEI number by registering in SAM.gov. 
 
35. Can an individual who is a lawful permanent resident with a Green Card be eligible to 
serve as Principal Investigator on a NIAC proposal/grant? 
Please note that NASA’s policy on grant and cooperative agreement proposals involving 
non-U.S. participation (as stated in Section 9.4 of the NASA Grant and Cooperative 
Agreement Manual [GCAM]) applies to organizations, not individuals. 
Thus, this policy would not prevent a legal permanent resident from being proposed as a 
PI on a NIAC proposal; that decision would reside with the submitting organization 
(provided it is a U.S. Organization). Please note that this assessment does not factor in 
whether or not the organization would be in compliance with any other requirements or 
restrictions in the solicitation, GCAM, or terms and conditions, such as export control.

47. For an individual who originates a NIAC concept and partners with a university lab to

48. Can we assume a hypothetical facility to support the mission? 
It is acceptable to propose a hypothetical facility to assist in making the case for your 
innovation and how it can be accommodated within a mission context. Ensure that the 
interfaces and boundaries between your part of the proposal and the presumed facility are 
clear. Also, ensure that the existence case for the facility is reasonable and logical, even 
if it does not currently exist within a NASA timeline or appropriated budget. 
 
49. For concepts in which the primary innovation is in the (fill in the blank) technology area, 
what would distinguish an in-scope NIAC development from an out-of-scope systems-
engineering or algorithm-development tool? 
There must be some innovation involved that changes how current approaches to (fill in 
the blank) technologies are implemented today. As stated in Sections C.2. and C.3 the 
NIAC Appendix, “Innovations that simply exploit unrelated but anticipated future 
capability, without introducing a novel or unanticipated aspect may be considered 
incremental” and thus potentially out-of-scope for NIAC. 
 
50. Does NIAC consider concepts whose primary innovation is in crew capability or human-
system performance, as opposed to vehicle, instrument, or mission architecture, to be 
within scope, and which directorate's reviewers evaluate them? 
Yes. Innovation in human-system performance and crew capabilities and efficiencies are 
acceptable topics for a NIAC Phase I proposal effort. NIAC uses a variety of reviewers to 
achieve best coverage of the received topics. 
 
51. Should the proposal include a specific NASA mission (and if yes, does it require any 
prior agreement with the corresponding unit at NASA) or can it be more broadly defined? 
There is no requirement to reference a specific NASA mission that is planned, budgeted, 
and being implemented. The proposal does need to describe an innovation that is

presented in the context of some mission that has relevant to space, exploration, science, 
aeronautics, or other NASA domain. It is the purpose of the proposal to describe how the 
innovation greatly improves the utilizing mission in some way, whether the mission is 
"on the books" at NASA or not. 
 
52. At the Step A stage, what level of technical substantiation most helps reviewers 
distinguish a genuinely promising NIAC concept from an interesting but still speculative 
idea? For example, should Step A already contain first-order physics, quantitative 
feasibility bounds, key assumptions, and explicit technical risks, or should much of that 
depth be reserved for Step B? 
The bulk of technical depth used to substantiate a promising and innovative concept is 
reserved for the Step B proposal. But Step A proposals must also contain enough 
information, back-of-the-envelope calculations, and context such that the reviewer has 
confidence that the proposer has done the calculations required to ensure the concept will 
work and can defend the innovation in a Step B proposal. 
 
53. The Appendix states that NIAC Phase I concepts are "generally aimed ten or more years 
in the future." Is that intended as a firm expectation for the earliest plausible 
implementation of the concept, or primarily as a characterization of the type of high-risk, 
transformative research NIAC seeks? Could a concept remain appropriate if a proposer 
believes an aggressive development program might make implementation possible in 3 or 
5 years, if the substantial feasibility questions are resolved, where resolving them is the 
point of our proposal to NIAC? 
The "ten years in the future" description generally is intended to serve as a 
characterization of the type of high-risk, transformative research NIAC seeks. If a 
proposal can convince the review panel that an innovation is capable of providing 
substantial benefit within a time frame sooner than ten years, possibly even today, then it 
could make an acceptable NIAC study. 
 
54. Do the hardware ideas need to be technologically advancing or could they be 
architectural, enabling new persistent operations? 
There is an example in how the iPhone was developed and introduced by Apple. The 
first version of the iPhone did not introduce new capabilities. Instead, it integrated many 
existing available capabilities into a single device that, due to its consolidation of 
functions, provided a new capability in how we perform tasks in our daily lives. NIAC is 
interested in proposals that provide some innovation in architecture or utilization that 
could "change the possible" by introducing some new persistent operational change in 
how missions are carried out - even if the underlying technologies are known and mature. 
 
55. For a Phase I study, particularly for concepts involving autonomy, software, sensing, or 
system architecture, what level of validation is normally expected by the end of the 
award? Would analytical modelling and simulation be sufficient, or are software

prototypes, hardware-in-the-loop demonstrations, or physical experiments generally 
expected? 
As a study, the Phase I effort is not required to produce prototypes, hardware-in-the-loop 
demonstrations, or physical experiments to validate the innovation being developed. 
That being said, the review panels do tend to look favorably on "metal-bending" during 
the course of the study, but only to the extent that it informs the overall mission concept

56. Is a 9-month Phase I study expected to be analysis and simulation only, or may it include 
a small human-subjects or laboratory pilot? 
Past Phase I projects have sometimes included lab-based experimental results, but this is 
not a requirement for a Phase I proposal. 
 
57. For a reusable lunar transportation concept that depends on a fixed surface installation 
and mobile vehicles, how should Step A quantify mission-level benefit? Should the 
comparison include deployment mass and power for the complete system, alongside per-
sortie performance over an explicitly stated number of missions? 
A representative mission involves the architecture, science objectives, operations, 
logistics, system-level design, etc. that frames the innovation being proposed. NIAC 
studies provide the opportunity to convince NASA that an underlying technology concept 
would be worth further development by showing that it enables something new, 
substantially better, or more cost effective. If the proposer believes that is 
accomplishable with per-sortie performance over some number of missions, then that 
should be included in the proposal. For Step A, an initial back-of-the-envelope estimate 
on size, weight, and power would be beneficial. 
 
58. What sort of specifics are desired regarding the "exemplar mission" description? Would it 
be better to say "Artemis 6", or would the panel be looking for descriptive nomenclature 
such as "lunar mission, done in habitat?" 
Reviewers are agnostic as to how you title or describe your mission. It is the proposer’s 
responsibility to describe a representative mission that best highlights the benefits of the 
proposed concept or technology. 
 
59. Can a proposal address innovation in multiple areas (for example, a new propulsion 
system and a new robotic system)? If yes, what can the image on the summary chart look 
like? 
A proposal may address multiple innovations, but that would seem to defocus the point of 
the proposal and is not recommended. The image on the summary chart should represent 
the focus of the proposal in a mission context. If the proposed innovations are not related, 
one might consider proposing two separate Step A proposals. 
 
60. If the innovation is an in-space production facility (e.g., an orbital plant manufacturing a 
consumable for other missions), may the facility's own operation serve as the single

## Page 13

representative mission with its system mass, power, and volume estimated? Alternatively, 
should the representative mission be a downstream mission that uses the product, treating 
the facility as the enabling system? 
The facility's operation of product production should not serve as the mission context, but 
rather the application or usage of the product being manufactured should serve as the 
mission context - with evidence on how that product will enable new, novel, innovative 
capabilities for the mission. 
 
61. When full-scale implementation requires a very large program, will an incremental 
path—where each early step provides a complete, independently useful capability—
satisfy the "plausible approach" requirement under the programmatic credibility 
criterion? 
Proposers are encouraged to consult the criterion listed in the solicitation and propose 
what they feel would be received best by cognizant reviewers accordingly. An 
incremental approach is deemed plausible if it is well justified by the innovation. 
 
62. For a propulsion concept reliant on a consumable not yet available at the required scale, 
is it acceptable to state this assumption explicitly and summarize a separate supply 
analysis? Or must the Phase I study itself assess the supply's feasibility to meet the 
programmatic credibility criterion? 
In Step A you should reference this assumption, whereas in Step B it would be 
appropriate to discuss and justify why this assumption is being made. Some initial 
estimate or quantification of any currently unavailable consumable is warranted. 
 
63. For a Phase I power-system concept, how deep should the end-to-end mission analysis 
go? For example, should fuel sourcing, power delivery, and environmental effects like 
dust be analyzed in detail, or is it acceptable to address them at a high level with stated 
assumptions and mitigations, and focus the study on the feasibility of the core 
technology? 
The focus should be on the core technology, but there also needs to be some indication 
that the proposer understands the mission context in which the innovation is being 
proposed. There should be some information about spacecraft bus power supply, 
spacecraft mass and the innovation's influence on mass changes, fuel, power, and 
environmental effects to the degree that they affect the success of the innovation. 
 
64. If the underlying concept could support several future NASA missions, is it preferable to 
anchor the proposal around one specific reference mission and quantify its performance 
there, while treating other applications as extensions? Or can the Phase I study evaluate 
several mission scenarios? 
The proposal should focus on one example mission that the innovation would benefit, but 
also identify the other missions that could derive benefit should the proposed innovation 
become mature and utilized.

65. Can we ensure reviewers take seriously mid-term commercial launch capacity plans, 
instead of assuming anything not doable with 2005 launch capability would be 
infeasible? 
It is certainly allowable and even encouraged to presume that federal and commercial 
heavy-lift launch capabilities will exist into the future such that an innovation and its 
mission context are doable. But understand that the NIAC program is not looking for 
proposals that simply presume heavy lift is the innovation and now enables some 
capability that could not be done previously because of launched mass constraints. There 
needs to be some other innovation and mission concept that may use a heavy-lift 
capability, but not one that claims the heavy-lift capability is the innovation. 
 
66. For the Phase I expectation that the entry TRL be 2 or lower, is the relevant TRL that of 
the specific integrated concept being proposed, or must all major enabling subsystems

architecture be appropriate for NIAC if it incorporates one or more enabling technologies

subsystems? 
The Technology Readiness Level of an innovation or technology is of lesser importance 
to the program than the innovation of the concept and its incorporation in a mission 
context. It is not a disqualifying situation to have some element of the proposal be rated 
at a TRL 3 or above. 
 
67. Are proposals that enter Phase II and Phase III meant to be beyond TRL 2? 
The Technology Readiness Level of an innovation or technology is of lesser importance 
to the program than the innovation of the concept and its incorporation in a mission

68. In Step A, can you quantify the elimination criteria "not technically credible" as a TRL 
i.e. TRL 1 or not even TRL 1? 
The NIAC program is not likely to use the TRL of a proposed innovation as a benchmark 
for selection or non-selection. If the proposed innovation seems to violate the laws of 
physics or engineering, then it may be deemed "not technically credible." More than the 
TRL of the innovation, reviewers are interested in the innovation's ability to move space 
exploration in new and positive directions, its ability to "change the possible", and its 
uniqueness among multiple solutions.
