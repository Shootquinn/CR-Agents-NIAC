C. Program Description

1. Overview The NASA Innovative Advanced Concepts (NIAC) Program focuses on early-stage exploration of visionary concepts in alignment with national space priorities and commercial aerospace goals. Consistent with Science: A New Golden Age and as explicitly stated in the accompanying OMB Memorandum NSTM-5 / M-26-16, foundational research including basic and use-inspired inquiry across the sciences and engineering remains the bedrock of American scientific and technological leadership. Accordingly, NIAC solicits concepts from any field that offers a radically different approach or disruptive innovation that may significantly enhance or enable new human or robotic aerospace missions. NIAC is particularly interested in concepts that are creative and unexplored; ideas that have been explored, but not yet applied, are generally not appropriate unless there is a novel aspect that significantly increases its benefit or feasibility.

The NIAC Program supports innovative research through multiple phases of award. Phase I awards are nine-month efforts funded at up to $225k, to explore the overall feasibility and viability of visionary concepts. Phase II awards are for up to two years at a total funding level of up to $750k per award, to further develop the most promising Phase I concepts, and to explore potential infusion options within and beyond NASA. Concepts should be sufficiently advanced by the end of Phase II to pursue follow‑on development funds from government or commercial programs external to NIAC, building on the transition planning and partnerships encouraged during Phase I. Phase III awards are designed to meet the strategic investment of additional NIAC funds to further advance Phase II technologies with a clearly defined

transition paths into other NASA, government, or commercial programs. Phase III studies will be funded for a maximum duration of two years at a total funding level of up to $2M per award (i.e., in approximately equal annual allocations).

NIAC seeks to foster a community of innovators whose interactions and collaborations may persist well beyond the performance of the Phase I award. NIAC award recipients are required to participate in a Phase I Orientation Meeting soon after award, and at a n annual Symposium in September where all active NIAC Fellows present the status of their research in a publicly open forum. At the completion of the NIAC project, a final report is required, and this report serves as the primary measure for evaluating pro ject performance. The final report must document the progress, outcomes, and research products resulting from the effort, and it will be posted on the publicly available NIAC website.

2. Phase I Goals and Objectives Within the Advanced Research and Technology Division of NASA’s Research and Technology Mission Directorate, NASA seeks through NIAC exciting, unexplored, credible aerospace concepts with at least one mission application. NIAC focuses on early exploration of visionary aerospace concepts proposed in the context of a representative mission.

NIAC Phase I concepts typically will advance from TRL 1-2 in maturity (please refer to NASA TRL definitions) and are generally aimed ten or more years in the future. Accordingly, this Appendix supports the Executive Order 14369—Ensuring American Space Superiority priority: “advanced capabilities and approaches that enable the next century of space achievements.” To complement NASA’s optimization of “space research-and-development investments to achieve my Administration's near-term space objectives,” NIAC specifically aims to leverage “emerging technologies and scientific discoveries to advance mission capabilities, and enable scientific discovery for America’s long-term science and technology leadership.” Looking well beyond currently planned missions, NIAC seeks to inform future roadmaps and inspire new mission paradigms. NIAC is thus uniquely postured among NASA technology programs to meet the Agency’s commitment to accomplish the near-impossible.

This Appendix therefore seeks visionary concepts across the spectrum of aerospace advancement, including in emerging areas consistent with the Administration research and development priority areas referenced in OMB Memorandum NSTM-5 / M-26-16. Examples of such areas include novel applications of AI, quantum sensing, advanced nuclear technologies, and robotics.

NIAC is unique in that it requires the exploration of a specific representative mission to demonstrate the feasibility and benefit of the innovation. A general list of missions that could benefit from the innovation is not sufficient. A representative mission depends on the concept, but generally involves the architecture, objectives, operations, logistics, system-level design, etc. that frame the particular innovation being proposed. The following examples are meant to be representative, not exclusive or limiting:

• Advanced space transportation concepts must include assessments of the full vehicle mass and payload for an identified destination. • Instrument or observatory concepts demonstrate that the instrument enables or substantially improves science exploration and fits within or operates in conjunction with a spacecraft or system capable of supporting its operation and data collection in the intended environment. • Life support and other human health concepts identify the mission application (lunar, Mars transit, Mars surface, etc.) and show that the inclusion of the innovation has a substantial benefit over alternatives, after accounting for the equivalent system mass needed to enable it. • Advanced robotic concepts show the innovation supports one or more needs, with reasonable and attainable mass, power, and volume consistent with a selected specific mission objective • Advanced power concepts identify the benefit of the new power concept, and assesses the amount of power, the specific power, and the mass of the system that would deliver the power in the exemplar mission context for a specified length of time.

Any assumptions about the overall context of a mission should be explicitly noted, such as availability of abundant power or other infrastructure already in place. For example, does it assume that an extensive in situ resource architecture is already in place or is it intending to take advantage of a future large lift volume or mass enabled by heavy launch vehicles under development. Innovations that simply exploit unrelated but anticipated future capability, without introducing a novel or unanticipated aspect may be considered incremental or evolutionary. Similarly, concepts that simply note the advantages of in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) will not be considered innovative but must show a substantially new and unexplored approach to exploit ISRU.

NIAC requires a preliminary “back of the envelope” calculation to justify feasibility of the innovation and a preliminary “back of the envelope” calculation to justify the impact of the concept in an exemplar mission. These are necessary in the proposal (Step A and Step B, as described below in section F) to show why the proposer believes the concept could potentially change the possible in a future space or aeronautics mission. It is recognized that the estimation cannot be too refined without the NIAC Phase I.

NIAC Phase I efforts are intended to be a stand-alone assessment of the feasibility and benefit of an innovative concept in a representative mission. References to or discussion of what

3. Phase I Concept Requirements Comparatively high risk and far-term, NIAC concepts are transformational investments in future NASA and commercial capabilities. Concepts must demonstrate an unexplored and highly innovative approach framed within a representative mission that clearly identifies scientific or technical advancements and associated benefits compared to current capabilities. The entry Technology Readiness Level (TRL) for Phase I concepts should be TRL 2 or lower.

Phase I efforts are intended to be stand-alone assessments addressing the most significant feasibility issues of the proposed concept in the context of the representative mission. Concepts proposed in Phase I must satisfy the following criteria to qualify as candidates for a NIAC Phase I award. The proposed concept must:

• Advance Aeronautics and Space Innovation - Pursues innovations in air and space applications, contributing to enhanced capability, security, and sustained U.S. leadership and superiority in space and aeronautics. • Be Innovative and Offer High Potential Impact - Enables an entirely new mission, greatly improves mission execution, or offers a significant leap in capabilities. - Breaks new ground, changing the conversation about future mission possibilities, or significantly contributing to scientific knowledge and understanding. - There are demonstrable benefits to conducting the project now, even if the ultimate vision is considered far-term or high risk. - Generally, a NIAC concept is based on new technology or science, but it can also be based on existing technology or science applied to a mission in significantly new and innovative ways. - Innovations that simply exploit unrelated but anticipated future capability, without introducing a novel or unanticipated aspect may be considered incremental. - Ideas that have been explored, but not yet applied, are generally not appropriate unless there is a novel aspect that significantly increases its benefit or feasibility • Be Credible and Reasonable - Technically sound; based on solid scientific/engineering principles. - Plausibly implementable; at least one reasonable path for further development and eventual implementation must be described and substantiated by a back of the envelope calculation. • Be Proposed in the Context of a Representative Mission - A representative mission involves the entire framework (i.e., including, science objectives, operations, logistics, system -level design, etc.) surrounding the

innovation being proposed. Innovative technologies, such as an instrument or detector, should not be submitted as a standalone development to NIAC. Rather, they should be incorporated within a specific representative mission. A list of missions that could benefit from the innovation is not sufficient. - A mission is a plan to achieve one or more clear objectives (e.g., advancing exploration, science, aerospace operations, aeronautics) to benefit NASA or the larger aerospace community. - NIAC concepts must be incorporated into a well -defined representative mission to contextualize the innovation for development and analysis. A description of where the mission is applicable should be included. - To highlight the innovation and its potential, proposers must provide a rudimentary comparison with alternative approaches within the proposal and outline a more extensive approach in the proposed work plan. - The representative mission need not be a current or planned NASA mission. Any aerospace mission (previously considered or hypothetical) may be selected. - The innovation must be described in terms of the impact on an exemplar mission after launch. A focus on the development of systems engineering tools or processes applied in early stages of mission development to improve design, decision-making, or algorithm development without sufficient incorporation into the flight phase of the representative mission is not appropriate for a NIAC concept.

To summarize, NIAC projects provide the opportunity to convince NASA and the broader aerospace community that an underlying technology concept would be worth further development by showing that it enables something new, substantially better, or more cost effective.

m. Security Requirements for Unmanned Aircraft System (UAS) Procured Using NASA Grant or Cooperative Agreement Funding (Applicable to proposals that actively employ UAS only)

The American Security Drone Act (ASDA) is a federal law enacted as part of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2024. It bans federal agencies, federal contractors, and recipients of federal funds from purchasing or operating unmanned aircraft systems (drones) manufactured or assembled by designated foreign entities tied to national security concerns. This prohibition extends to the use of previously purchased foreign UAS in current research or data generated by previously purchased foreign drones.

Background & Applicability Office of Management and Budget (OMB) memorandum M-26-02, Ensuring Government Use of Secure Unmanned Aircraft Systems and Supporting United States Producers, requires Federal agencies to apply appropriate safeguards for the protection of Federal information that is generated by or otherwise accessible to unmanned aircraft systems (UAS), including those procured using Federal grant and cooperative agreement (hereinafter “grant”) funds.

Any grant issued under this NOFO that will fund the procurement of UAS that process, store, or transmit Federal information is required to adhere to the security requirements described below. Applicants that intend to procure such UAS must clearly and explicitly describe the intended procurement in their budget and budget narrative and describe the intended procurement in the Scientific/Technical/Management Plan section of their proposal or other applicable section as described in a NOFO or NOFO appendix. After proposal submission, NASA may request that additional information pertaining to the proposed UAS procurement be provided so that NASA may conduct an impact assessment per requirements in M-26-02.

For the purposes of this NOFO, “Unmanned aircraft system” has the definition found in Title 48 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), section 40.201, Definitions: “an unmanned aircraft and associated elements (including communication links and the components that control the unmanned aircraft) that are required for the operator to operate safely and efficiently in the national airspace system (49 U.S.C. 44801(12)).”

“Federal information” is defined as “information created, collected, processed, maintained, disseminated, disclosed, or disposed of by or for the Federal Government, in any medium

Security Requirements for UAS Applicants proposing to use NASA grant funds to procure UAS that will process, store, or transmit Federal information must describe how they will manage the UAS in accordance with the information security requirements listed below. Applicants must also describe how they will develop a risk-based approach to applying these requirements to procurement solicitations to potential UAS vendors.

1. Access Control Requirements a. If personnel will remotely access UAS ground control stations, applicants should require and enforce appropriate authentication at the identification and authorization levels, including multifactor authentication per National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Special Publication (SP) 800-63, Digital Identify Guidelines, or any successor publication. b. If the overall system impact level for availability is moderate or high, applicants should consider whether the UAS program should be managed in accordance with an IT asset framework such as NIST SP 1800-5, IT Asset Management, or any successor publication.

2. Software and Firmware Update Requirements a. Software and firmware updates should come only from the UAS manufacturer or a trusted (as determined by the applicant’s procurement authorizing official) third- party. b. IT technology used for the installation and download of UAS software and firmware should be isolated from the applicant’s information systems. c. If the overall system impact level for integrity is moderate or high, operators should conduct a file integrity check and test firmware or software updates prior to mission operations.

3. Data Protection Requirements a. To the extent practicable, UAS Federal mission-related data should be encrypted at rest and during the collection and transmittal of such information. b. Sensitive data that is collected, stored, or processed by the UAS, or transmitted to or from it, should be cryptographically secured using approved and validated cryptographic algorithm and module. c. NASA retains the ability to opt out of any uploading, downloading, or transmitting of UAS data that is not required by law or regulation. When uploading, downloading, or transmitting data is required by law or regulation, NASA will preserve the ability to choose with whom information is shared and where it is stored, to the greatest extent practicable. See National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2024 (Pub. L. No 118-31 § 1829) for more information. d. If the UAS stores or processes sensitive data, applicants should consider acquiring a UAS with remote security capabilities (e.g., remote wipe or lock). Ideally, the operator should be able to trigger these remote security capabilities without manufacturer involvement.
