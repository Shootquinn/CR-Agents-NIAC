## NIAC advice for the Step A body

Rules for the Writer and the author. Each rule bears on the 3-page Step A body.

1. Frame the technology inside a mission context. Never present it as standalone technology. (niacfellows home page; step-a-proposals-how-to)
2. Make the concept stand on its own in the mission context. Do not lean on team capability for credibility. (proposal-process-overview)
3. Order the draft: need for the mission or science first, then the technology solution, then the mission concept. (how-to-get-niac-funding)
4. Write the draft like creative writing, with bracketed placeholders for literature citations and figures. (how-to-get-niac-funding)
5. Think like a mission planner, and also like an engineer or inventor. (how-to-get-niac-funding)
6. Keep Step A to three pages. Include no budget, staffing or management information. (how-to-get-niac-funding; step-a-proposals-how-to)
7. Use at most three hand-drawn diagrams (four if small) or generated artwork. (how-to-get-niac-funding)
8. Show within three pages that the technology and the mission are both plausible and possible. (step-a-proposals-how-to)
9. Write the outline with topic sentences, and make each topic sentence a testable claim. (use-ai-to-write)
10. Balance the proposal across three roughly equal parts: visionary (sci-fi), technical (credible), and gamesmanship (standing out among 250+ proposals for about 12 awards). (use-ai-to-write)
11. Remove em dashes and run a proofing pass with Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" guide. (use-ai-to-write)
12. Read the two example Step A proposals, CLOVER and ACTION, on the funding page. (how-to-get-niac-funding)
13. While the solicitation is open, advise no contact with NIAC staff. Questions go to the published FAQ. (how-to-get-niac-funding; proposal-process-overview)

## Nine elimination criteria

Screen the draft against each one before submission. (proposal-process-overview)

1. Not an aerospace architecture (weak national or commercial benefit).
2. Unclear concept.
3. No representative mission.
4. Previously explored.
5. Incremental work instead of a breakthrough.
6. Not technically credible.
7. Not programmatically credible.
8. Too narrowly focused.
9. Pre-mission tools developed without a representative mission.
